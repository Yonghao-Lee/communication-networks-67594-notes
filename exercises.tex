\section{Delay and switching}

\q{1.1}{1}{KR 1.P6}
Hosts A and B are $m$ metres apart, joined by one link of rate $R$ bit/s with propagation speed $s$. A sends one packet of $L$ bits starting at $t=0$.
(a) Write $d_{\text{prop}}$, $d_{\text{trans}}$ and the end-to-end delay (no processing or queuing).
(b) At $t=d_{\text{trans}}$, where is the last bit?
(c) If $d_{\text{prop}}>d_{\text{trans}}$, where is the first bit at $t=d_{\text{trans}}$?
(d) With $s=2.5\times10^8$\,m/s, $L=1500$ bytes and $R=10$\,Mbps, for which $m$ is $d_{\text{prop}}=d_{\text{trans}}$?

\q{1.2}{2}{KR 1.P10}
A 1500-byte packet crosses three links joined by two switches. Every link has $R=2.5$\,Mbps and $s=2.5\times10^8$\,m/s; the links are 5000, 4000 and 1000\,km long.
Each switch adds a processing delay of 3\,ms. There is no queuing. Find the end-to-end delay.

\q{1.3}{1}{KR 1.P12}
A packet arrives at a router. On its outgoing link one packet is half transmitted and four more are waiting. All packets are 1500 bytes and the link is 2.5\,Mbps.
(a) What is the queuing delay of the new packet? (b) Give the general formula when $x$ bits of the current packet are already sent and $n$ packets wait.

\q{1.4}{2}{KR 1.P31}
A message of $10^6$ bits goes from a source through two store-and-forward switches to a destination (three links, 5\,Mbps each). Ignore propagation, queuing and processing.
(a) How long does it take without segmentation?
(b) Now it is cut into 100 packets of 10\,000 bits. When does the first packet reach the first switch? When is the second packet fully received there?
(c) How long does the whole message take now? Why is it faster?

\q{1.5}{1}{own}
A 1\,000\,000-bit file is sent over a circuit-switched path. Every link is a 2\,Mbps TDM link with 20 slots, and setting up the circuit takes 300\,ms.
How long until the whole file has arrived (ignore propagation)? Does it depend on the number of links?

\q{1.6}{1}{own}
An application has peak rate 1\,Mbps and average rate 50\,kbps. (a) What is its utilization under circuit switching? (b) How many such applications fit on a 10\,Mbps link
with circuit switching? (c) How many would fit if each needed only its average rate?

\q{1.7}{1}{own}
Packets of 1000 bits arrive at a 1\,Mbps link at $a=900$ packets/s on average. (a) What is the traffic intensity? (b) Up to which arrival rate is the queue stable?

\q{1.8}{2}{KR 1.P8}
Users share a 10\,Mbps link. Each needs 200\,kbps when active and is active 10\% of the time, independently.
(a) How many users does circuit switching support?
(b) With packet switching and 120 users, what is the probability that exactly $n$ users are active?
(c) What is the probability that 51 or more are active? What does that mean for packet switching?

\q{1.9}{2}{KR 1.P9}
Same as Q1.8 but with a 1\,Gbps link and users of 100\,kbps, each active with probability $p=0.1$.
(a) What is $N$, the number of users under circuit switching? (b) With packet switching and $M$ users, write the probability that more than $N$ are active.

\section{Probability}

\q{1.10}{2}{T1 / lecture Example II}
Good packets survive $t$ seconds with probability $e^{-t}$, bad ones with probability $e^{-1000t}$. A packet is good with probability $0.2$.
(a) What is the probability that a packet survives $2$\,ms? (b) It survived $2$\,ms: what is the probability that it is good?

\q{1.11}{1}{KR 1.P22}
A path has 5 links, each losing a packet independently with probability $0.05$. The server resends a lost packet end to end.
(a) Probability that one transmission arrives? (b) Expected number of \emph{re}transmissions?

\q{1.12}{3}{T1 / lecture Example III}

(a) With end-to-end retransmission, what is the expected number of attempts by S, and the expected number of \emph{link} transmissions?
(b) With hop-by-hop retransmission, what is the expected number of link transmissions? Which scheme is cheaper?

\q{1.13}{2}{own}
Packets arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=3$ per second.
(a) Probability of no packet in 1\,s? (b) Probability of exactly 2 packets in 0.5\,s?
(c) You have already waited 2\,s without a packet. What is the expected remaining wait? Why?

\q{1.14}{1}{T1}
In each interval of length $T$ the number of packets sent is $\text{Pois}(gT)$. (a) What is the probability of exactly one packet? (b) For which $gT$ is it largest, and what is the largest value?

\s{1.1}
(a) $d_{\text{prop}}=m/s$, $d_{\text{trans}}=L/R$, end-to-end $=L/R+m/s$.
(b) It is just leaving A. (c) The first bit is still on the link, $s\cdot d_{\text{trans}}$ metres from A; it has not reached B.
(d) $m=s\cdot L/R=2.5\times10^8\cdot12\,000/10^7=2.5\times10^8\cdot1.2\times10^{-3}=300$\,km.

\s{1.2}
Each link: $L/R=12\,000/2.5\times10^6=4.8$\,ms; three links: $14.4$\,ms (store-and-forward transmits on every link).
Propagation: $20+16+4=40$\,ms. Processing: $2\times3=6$\,ms. Total $=14.4+40+6=$ \textbf{60.4\,ms}.

\s{1.3}
(a) Half a packet plus four packets: $(0.5+4)\times4.8$\,ms $=$ \textbf{21.6\,ms}. (b) $\dfrac{nL+(L-x)}{R}$.

\s{1.4}
(a) Each link takes $10^6/(5\times10^6)=0.2$\,s; store-and-forward over 3 links: \textbf{0.6\,s}.
(b) One packet per link: $10^4/(5\times10^6)=2$\,ms. The first packet reaches switch 1 at $2$\,ms; the second is fully received there at $4$\,ms.
(c) $(N+K-1)\,L/R=(3+100-1)\times2$\,ms $=$ \textbf{204\,ms}, about 3 times faster: the links work in parallel (a pipeline) instead of waiting for the whole message.

\s{1.5}
Each circuit gets $2\,\text{Mbps}/20=100$\,kbps, so transmission takes $10^6/10^5=10$\,s. Total $=10+0.3=$ \textbf{10.3\,s}.
It does not depend on the number of links: the bits stream through the reserved path without store-and-forward.

\s{1.6}
(a) $A/P=50/1000=$ \textbf{5\%}. (b) Reserve the peak: $10/1=$ \textbf{10}. (c) $10\,000/50=$ \textbf{200}. Statistical multiplexing aims at something close to (c).

\s{1.7}
(a) $La/R=1000\cdot900/10^6=$ \textbf{0.9}. (b) It must stay below 1: $a<10^6/1000=$ \textbf{1000} packets/s. At 0.9 the queue is stable but the queuing delay is already large.

\s{1.8}
(a) $10\,\text{Mbps}/200\,\text{kbps}=$ \textbf{50} users.
(b) $X\sim\text{Bin}(120,0.1)$: $P(X=n)=\binom{120}{n}0.1^n0.9^{120-n}$.
(c) $P(X\ge51)=\sum_{n=51}^{120}\binom{120}{n}0.1^n0.9^{120-n}\approx2\times10^{-20}$. The mean is 12 and the standard deviation $\sqrt{120\cdot0.1\cdot0.9}\approx3.3$, so 51 is about 12 standard deviations away.
Packet switching serves 120 users where circuit switching serves 50, and the link is practically never overloaded.

\s{1.9}
(a) $N=10^9/10^5=$ \textbf{10\,000}. (b) $P(X>N)=\sum_{k=N+1}^{M}\binom Mk p^k(1-p)^{M-k}$, with $X\sim\text{Bin}(M,p)$.

\s{1.10}
Let $A$ = ``survives 2\,ms'', $B$ = ``good''.
(a) Total probability: $P(A)=0.2e^{-0.002}+0.8e^{-2}=0.1996+0.1083=$ \textbf{0.308}.
(b) Bayes: $P(B\mid A)=0.1996/0.308=$ \textbf{0.648}. Surviving raised the chance of ``good'' from $0.2$ to $0.65$.

\s{1.11}
(a) All 5 links must work (independent, so multiply): $0.95^5=$ \textbf{0.774}.
(b) Attempts $\sim\text{Geo}(0.774)$, mean $1/0.774=1.292$, so on average \textbf{0.29} retransmissions ($=$ attempts $-1$).

\s{1.12}
(a) An attempt succeeds with probability $0.8^4=0.4096$, so $\E[\text{attempts}]=1/0.4096=$ \textbf{2.44}.
An attempt uses link $k$ only if links $1..k-1$ worked: $\E[\text{links per attempt}]=1+0.8+0.64+0.512=2.952$. Total $2.44\times2.952\approx$ \textbf{7.2} link transmissions.
(b) Each hop needs $\text{Geo}(0.8)$ sends, mean $1.25$; four hops: \textbf{5}. Hop-by-hop is cheaper because a loss only repeats one link, not the whole path.

\s{1.13}
(a) $\text{Pois}(3)$: $e^{-3}=$ \textbf{0.050}. (b) $\text{Pois}(1.5)$: $e^{-1.5}\,1.5^2/2=$ \textbf{0.251}.
(c) \textbf{1/3\,s}. Inter-arrival times are $\text{Exp}(3)$, which is memoryless: the 2\,s already waited change nothing.

\s{1.14}
(a) $P(X=1)=gT\,e^{-gT}$. (b) The derivative $(1-gT)e^{-gT}$ is zero at $gT=1$, where the value is $1/e\approx$ \textbf{0.368}. This is slotted ALOHA's best goodput (week 2).

\q{2.9}{3}{R2 Q1 variant}

\q{2.10}{3}{R2 Q2 variant}

\s{2.1}
(a) $200/(200+54)=$ \textbf{78.7\%}. (b) $200/(200+54+4+8)=200/266=$ \textbf{75.2\%}.

\s{2.2}
HTTP: 7 (application). TLS: 6 (presentation). TCP ACKs: 4 (transport). IP routing: 3 (network). CSMA/CD: 2 (data link, MAC sub-layer). Fibre: 1 (physical).
In the Internet stack, session (5) and presentation (6) are not separate layers: the application provides them, so TLS counts as ``application''.

\s{2.3}
(a) $40/8=$ \textbf{5\,Mbps} each, even though more is free. (b) $40-3\cdot5=$ \textbf{25\,Mbps} idle.
(c) Property 2: $M=3$ busy nodes should get $R/M\approx13.3$\,Mbps each. (Property 1 also fails: a lone node would get only $R/8$.)

\s{2.4}
(a) $\frac{d}{dp}Np(1-p)^{N-1}=N(1-p)^{N-2}[(1-p)-(N-1)p]=0\Rightarrow p^*=1/N$. Maximum $(1-\frac1N)^{N-1}\to$ \textbf{$1/e\approx0.37$}.
(b) $\frac{d}{dp}Np(1-p)^{2N-2}=N(1-p)^{2N-3}[(1-p)-(2N-2)p]=0\Rightarrow p^*=\frac1{2N-1}$.
Maximum $\frac{N}{2N-1}(1-\frac1{2N-1})^{2N-2}\to\frac12\cdot\frac1e=$ \textbf{$1/(2e)\approx0.18$}.

\s{2.5}
A wins a given slot with probability $q=p(1-p)^3$ ($=0.1055$ at $p=0.25$), and slots are independent.
(a) Fail 3 times, then win: $(1-q)^3q=$ \textbf{0.0755}.
(b) Exactly one of four: $4p(1-p)^3=$ \textbf{0.422}.
(c) Let $s=4p(1-p)^3$. No success in slots 1--3, then success: $(1-s)^3s=0.578^3\cdot0.422=$ \textbf{0.0815}.
(d) Efficiency $=s=$ \textbf{0.422} (here $p=1/N$, so this is the best possible for 4 nodes).

\s{2.6}
(a) A wins when it sends and B does not: $p_A(1-p_B)$. Total efficiency $=p_A(1-p_B)+p_B(1-p_A)$.
(b) No: $\dfrac{2p_B(1-p_B)}{p_B(1-2p_B)}=\dfrac{2(1-p_B)}{1-2p_B}>2$, because A's extra sending also blocks B more often.
Exactly twice needs $p_A(1-p_B)=2p_B(1-p_A)$, i.e.\ \textbf{$p_A=\dfrac{2p_B}{1+p_B}$}. Check with $p_B=0.1$: $p_A=0.182$; $0.182\cdot0.9=0.164=2\cdot0.1\cdot0.818$.
(c) A: $2p(1-p)^{N-1}$. Another node: it sends, A is silent, and the other $N-2$ are silent: $p(1-2p)(1-p)^{N-2}$.

\s{2.7}
(a) $T=L/B=2000/(2\times10^6)=$ \textbf{1\,ms}.
(b) $\eta=8\cdot0.1\cdot0.9^7=$ \textbf{0.383}.
(c) $1-0.9^8=$ \textbf{0.570}: the channel is busy 57\% of the time but useful only 38\%.
(d) $0.383\times2\,\text{Mbps}=$ \textbf{765\,kbps}. (e) $T/\eta=1/0.383=$ \textbf{2.6\,ms}.

\s{2.8}
(a) Success $Ge^{-G}=$ \textbf{0.268}; empty $e^{-G}=$ \textbf{0.670}; collision $1-0.670-0.268=$ \textbf{0.062}.
(b) $Ge^{-2G}=0.4e^{-0.8}=$ \textbf{0.180}.
(c) Slotted: increase towards $G^*=1$ (max $0.368$). Pure: decrease slightly, to $G^*=0.5$ (max $0.184$); $0.4$ is already near it.

\s{2.9}
(a) Total probability over the network chosen.
Upper (slotted): C sends and the 3 others are silent in that slot: $q(1-q)^3$.
Lower (pure): each of the 2 others must skip both of its overlapping slots: $q\big((1-q)^2\big)^2=q(1-q)^4$.
\[ P=q\big[p(1-q)^3+(1-p)(1-q)^4\big]=0.1\,[0.6\cdot0.729+0.4\cdot0.656]=\textbf{0.070}. \]
(b) If C picks the upper network (prob.\ $p$) there are 4 active nodes; otherwise 3. A slot succeeds if exactly one sends:
\[ \eta^{\text{upper}}=p\cdot4q(1-q)^3+(1-p)\cdot3q(1-q)^2=0.6\cdot0.292+0.4\cdot0.243=\textbf{0.272}. \]
(Note the difference: (a) is about \emph{C}, one node, no $\binom n1$; (b) is about the \emph{network}, any node.)

\s{2.10}
(a) The slotted node sends ($p$); the other $n-1$ slotted nodes are silent in this slot ($(1-p)^{n-1}$).
A pure frame of length $T$ starting anywhere in $(t-T,t+T)$ overlaps the slot; each pure node has exactly 2 slot starts there: $(1-p)^{2m}$.
\[ P_{\text{slotted}}=p(1-p)^{n-1}(1-p)^{2m}. \]
(b) The other pure nodes: $(1-p)^{2(m-1)}$, as in plain pure ALOHA. The pure frame is not aligned with the grid, so it always straddles \textbf{two} grid slots: all $n$ slotted nodes must skip both, $(1-p)^{2n}$.
\[ P_{\text{pure}}=p(1-p)^{2(m-1)}(1-p)^{2n}. \]
(c) Slotted: $0.1\cdot0.9^{14}=$ \textbf{0.0229}. Pure: $0.1\cdot0.9^{18}=$ \textbf{0.0150}. The slotted node does better: it is exposed to one slot of each slotted node, while the unaligned pure frame is exposed to two slots of everyone.

\s{2.11}
$\PROP=200/(2\times10^8)=1\,\mu$s. Need $\TRANSP\ge2\,\PROP=2\,\mu$s, so $L\ge2\times10^{-6}\cdot10^9=2000$ bits $=$ \textbf{250 bytes}.
Ethernet's 64 bytes would last only $0.512\,\mu$s at 1\,Gbps: too short to detect collisions over 200\,m. (Shared-medium gigabit Ethernet therefore had to extend short frames; in practice gigabit Ethernet is switched.)

\s{2.12}
After 5 collisions $K\in\{0,\dots,31\}$, uniform: $P(K=4)=$ \textbf{1/32}. Delay $=4\cdot512\cdot0.1\,\mu$s $=$ \textbf{204.8\,$\mu$s}.

\s{2.13}
$100\cdot512=51\,200$ bit times. At 100\,Mbps (10\,ns/bit): \textbf{512\,$\mu$s}. At 1\,Gbps (1\,ns/bit): \textbf{51.2\,$\mu$s}.

\s{2.14}
Worst case: B starts just before A's first bit arrives, at $t\approx325$. B's signal reaches A at $t\approx325+325=650$ bit times.
A finishes at $t=576<650$, so \textbf{yes}: A finishes before it hears B, and wrongly believes its frame got through. The cable is too long for this frame size ($\TRANSP<2\,\PROP$).

\s{2.15}
(a) $\{0,1,2,3\}$. (b) Same $K$: $4\cdot(1/4)^2=$ \textbf{1/4}.

\s{2.16}
$a=2/50=0.04$. (a) $1/(1+3a)=1/1.12=$ \textbf{0.893}; precise $1/(1+5a)=1/1.2=$ \textbf{0.833}.
(b) $A=(1-\alpha)/\alpha=e-1\approx1.72$, so $\eta=1/(1+2Aa)=1/(1+3.44\cdot0.04)=$ \textbf{0.879}.

\s{2.17}
(a) A contention slot is won with probability $\alpha=Np(1-p)^{N-1}$. The expected number of wasted slots before a win is $x=(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ (failures before a geometric success). Efficiency $=\dfrac{k}{k+x}=\dfrac{k}{k+(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$.
(b) Efficiency grows with $\alpha$, so maximize $\alpha$: \textbf{$p^*=1/N$}.
(c) $\alpha\to1/e$, $x\to e-1$: efficiency $\to$ \textbf{$\dfrac{k}{k+e-1}$}.
(d) $\dfrac{k}{k+e-1}\to$ \textbf{1}: long frames make the contention overhead negligible (the same as $a\to0$ in the lecture).

\s{2.18}
(a) $50\,\mu$s per $500\,\mu$s frame: \textbf{10\%}.
(b) Without RTS/CTS a collision wastes a whole data frame, $500\,\mu$s; with it only an RTS, $25\,\mu$s.
(c) When collisions are rare (light load) or frames are short: then the fixed $50\,\mu$s costs more than the collisions it prevents.
